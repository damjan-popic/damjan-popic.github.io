However, English final “voiced” consonants are not necessarily voiced throughout. The preceding vowel's duration and other cues help distinguish the words; final stop releases can also be weak or absent. The goal is **a recoverable word contrast**, not a forced vocal buzz or an added vowel after every consonant. Compare a dictionary recording of *code* with *coat*, and listen to the entire syllable. Do not produce *code-uh* to make the ending obvious. Šuštaršič's [contrastive account](https://www.internationalphoneticassociation.org/icphs-proceedings/ICPhS1999/p14_0763.html) discusses why the English fortis–lenis contrast cannot be reduced to the Slovene voicing pattern.

Consonant clusters also carry information: **script, scripts, text, texts, next take, first frame, asked, mixed**. Many Slavic languages already allow substantial clusters, so the existence of several adjacent consonants is not itself a Slavic-specific difficulty. Check the unfamiliar English combinations and the endings that mark grammar. Build *scripts* from *script + s*, then put it in a phrase: **three scripts**. Do not add a syllable between /p/, /t/ and /s/.

In fast speech, speakers may simplify clusters. Practise a careful form first, but do not require an artificial explosion of every stop in ordinary speech. A listener must still be able to distinguish **one script** from **three scripts** and **we mix** from **we mixed**. See the British Council's [cluster explanation and listening examples](https://africa.teachingenglish.org.uk/classroom/pronunciation/consonant-clusters).

#### Small endings that carry grammar: -s and -ed

The pronunciation of these endings depends primarily on the **last sound of the base**, not its last letter. The tables describe regular grammatical endings; individual lexical adjectives and irregular forms need separate checking.

| Regular -s/-es ending | Environment | Production vocabulary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| /s/ | After a voiceless non-sibilant consonant | *clips, tracks, takes* |
| /z/ | After a vowel or a voiced non-sibilant consonant | *cameras, files, frames* |
| /ɪz/ | After a sibilant: /s, z, ʃ, ʒ, tʃ, dʒ/ | *lenses, mixes, changes* |

Thus **lenses** belongs in the third group because the singular **lens** ends in /z/. The written ending alone cannot decide the answer. In the second row, *cameras, files* and *frames* do not gain a syllable. The vowel in the extra syllable of /ɪz/ can vary in actual speech; /ɪz/ is the broad teaching notation used here.

| Regular -ed ending | Environment | Production vocabulary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| /t/ | After a voiceless consonant other than /t/ | *checked, mixed, stopped, watched* |
| /d/ | After a vowel or voiced consonant other than /d/ | *filmed, played, moved, saved* |
| /ɪd/ | After /t/ or /d/; adds a syllable | *edited, recorded, wanted, needed* |

**Filmed** remains one syllable in this model; **edited** has three. Do not pronounce every *-ed* as a separate *ed*, and do not remove the ending merely because the cluster is unfamiliar. In **We checked the files**, the ending supplies grammatical information. For lexical exceptions, compare *a learned scholar* with *she learned the procedure*: the same letters do not guarantee the same spoken form. Dictionary audio for [filmed](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/dictionary/english/film), [edited](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/dictionary/english/edit) and [learned](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/dictionary/english/learned) provides useful checks.

#### /h/, /r/, /ŋ/ and aspiration

**English /h/** in *head, hand, headphone, ahead* is a breathy onset, not the strong back-of-the-mouth friction of a Slovene *h*-like /x/. Keep the difference between **heat** and **eat** when the word requires /h/. Conversely, **hour, honest** and **honour** have no initial /h/ in the common dictionary models. An article follows the next sound: **a headphone**, but **an hour**.

For a common English **/r/** model, the tongue approaches a constriction without tapping or repeatedly striking the roof of the mouth. A Slovene tap or trill may make an accent audible without making the word unintelligible; it is not the first thing to repair when the actual problem is a missing word or wrong number. Practise **record, room, reel, red** in a chosen model. General American is typically rhotic: the post-vocalic /r/ in *card* and *recorder* is pronounced; in many non-rhotic British models, post-vocalic /r/ is absent before a consonant or pause. Do not add or remove /r/ mechanically across accents.

In **sing, recording, lighting**, the final **/ŋ/** uses a closure at the back of the mouth with air passing through the nose. Do not automatically add /ɡ/ after it. **Singer** normally has /ŋ/ without /ɡ/ in the common models; **finger** normally has /ŋɡ/. Treat the word, not the spelling *ng*, as the unit to check.

At the beginning of a stressed syllable, **/p, t, k/** in common English models are usually accompanied by aspiration, a brief interval of breath before voicing starts: **take, camera, paper**. After /s/ in **stop, scan, speaker**, they are generally not aspirated in the same way. Compare **pin–spin** and **top–stop**. Aspiration is not a separately added /h/ syllable. These are optional refinement targets after important word contrasts, stress and endings are clear. Šuštaršič's [English–Slovene comparison](https://www.internationalphoneticassociation.org/icphs-proceedings/ICPhS1999/p14_0763.html) supplies the contrastive background.

#### Stress and weak syllables: do not give every vowel equal weight

**Word stress** makes one syllable more prominent than others. It involves a combination of duration, vowel quality, pitch and intensity, not simply shouting. Capital letters in the following **stress guides** identify the stressed part; these guides are not phonetic transcriptions or exact syllabification rules.

| Word or contrast | Stress guide | What changes? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *computer; equipment* | com-PU-ter; e-QUIP-ment | The first syllable is not the main stress. |
| *camera; microphone; monitor* | CAM-era; MI-cro-phone; MON-i-tor | The first syllable is prominent. |
| *a record / to record* | RE-cord / re-CORD | Noun and verb differ in their usual stress. |
| *a project / to project* | PRO-ject / pro-JECT | The noun naming a project differs from the verb meaning to project an image. |
| *photograph / photography / photographic* | PHO-to-graph / pho-TOG-ra-phy / pho-to-GRAPH-ic | Related words do not necessarily retain the same stress. |
| *cinematography* | cin-e-ma-TOG-ra-phy | Do not transfer the stress pattern of a familiar Slovene technical word. |
| *technician; production; resolution* | tech-NI-cian; pro-DUC-tion; res-o-LU-tion | Learn the entire spoken word, not a universal rule about an ending. |

Compare **We need a record of the sound** with **We need to record the sound**. Not every noun–verb pair shifts stress: *film* and *edit* do not follow the *record* pattern. A dictionary is more reliable than extrapolating from one example. See the British Council on [word stress](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/professional-development/teachers/knowing-subject/word-stress) and Cambridge on [photography](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/pronunciation/english/photography) and [cinematography](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/us/pronunciation/english/cinematography).

An unstressed vowel may be **schwa /ə/**, as at the beginning of **about** /əˈbaʊt/ and **computer** /kəmˈpjuːtə/ in a non-rhotic British model. Slovene has schwa too; what needs learning is its English distribution and relationship to stress. Do not pronounce every written vowel fully, but do not replace every unstressed vowel with schwa either. In **video** /ˈvɪdiəʊ/, the second vowel is not automatically /ə/. Listen to the actual word.

In connected speech, common grammatical words can have **weak forms**: *can* /kən/, *to* /tə/, *a* /ə/, and *and* /ən/ in suitable unstressed contexts. Compare an unstressed *can* in **We can record it** with the stressed reply **Yes, we CAN**. Weak forms are especially important for listening. In your own speech, retain clarity rather than trying to erase as many sounds as possible. English rhythm is not a metronome: stress intervals are not all exactly equal. See the British Council's explanations of [schwa](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/professional-development/teachers/teaching-knowledge-database/q-s/schwa) and [weak forms](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/professional-development/teachers/teaching-knowledge-database/t-w/weak-forms).

#### Sentence emphasis, pauses and numbers

Stress can identify the information being corrected:

> **Move the BLUE light.** — Not the red light.  
> **MOVE the blue light.** — Do not switch it off; change its position.  
> **Move the blue LIGHT.** — Not the blue reflector.

Practise the change in meaning, not merely the increase in volume. Use short thought groups: **After the interview / save the original files / and label the copy.** The slashes indicate useful spoken boundaries, not punctuation that must appear in the written instruction. A pause can help a listener understand which action or limitation belongs to which clause.

Do not claim that every question must rise in pitch or every statement must fall. Intonation depends on the kind of question, the intended attitude, the speaker and the variety of English. Ask a genuine clarification question rather than imitating an exaggerated “question voice”.

For critical information, clarity takes priority over reduction. In **Do NOT format the card**, keep the negative explicit; on a poor connection, **Do not** may be safer than an easily missed contraction. For **thirteen/thirty, fourteen/forty, fifteen/fifty**, retain the different ending and confirm digits when needed: **“Thirteen. One three.”** Stress may shift before another stressed word, so the rule is not simply “-teen always has final stress”. This connects with the number-reading and read-back examples in Chapter 1.

#### Tongue twisters: accuracy before speed

The following **twelve original classroom tongue twisters and articulation drills** use our course vocabulary. Some are deliberately repetitive. They are not authentic instructions or recommended technical procedures. Work individually: hear the lecturer's model, identify the target, say the line slowly, and repeat at a comfortable speaking pace. A recording can help you compare attempts. Stop speeding up when distinctions disappear. Being able to race through a tongue twister is not evidence that an ordinary explanation is clear.

| No. | Target | Say this |
| --- | --- | --- |
| T01 | /w/ and /v/ | **Vera views a very wide view while Will waves a white wire.** |
| T02 | /θ/ | **Three thin threads run through three thick theatre curtains.** |
| T03 | /ð/ and /d/ | **Those directors read those other directors' notes.** |
| T04 | /s/ and /ʃ/ | **Six sound assistants check six short shots.** |
| T05 | /ɪ/ and /iː/ | **The ship slips past the sheep; the sheep keep still.** |
| T06 | /æ/ and /ʌ/ | **Pack the black camera bag; pick up the cup by the truck.** |
| T07 | /r/ and /l/, in the chosen model | **Rory rolls the reel while Lily labels the little roll.** |
| T08 | Final clusters and grammatical endings | **Six script editors checked six scripts and mixed six tracks.** |
| T09 | Regular -ed endings | **We checked, filmed and edited; we stopped, moved and recorded.** |
| T10 | Stress-changing noun and verb | **Record the record; project the project; object to the object.** |
| T11 | /h/ and vowel-initial words | **Harry holds his headphones here for half an hour.** |
| T12 | /ŋ/ and /ŋɡ/ | **The singer keeps singing while a finger taps the ringing bell.** |

T10 is intentionally an articulation puzzle: *project the project* is not offered as the preferred wording for asking somebody to screen a film. Use the noun–verb pairs to hear the stress difference, then say a normal sentence such as **Please project the final sequence**.

For transfer to useful speech, finish each drill by explaining one real thing: what the viewfinder does, why a take was rejected, or which recording must be kept. Retain the target sounds without making the whole explanation sound like an exercise. The assessed aim is comprehensibility and control, not speed or the disappearance of your accent.
