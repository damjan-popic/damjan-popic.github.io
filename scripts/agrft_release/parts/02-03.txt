Distinguish **unless** from *if*: **Do not begin unless the lecturer has confirmed the setup** means that confirmation is a necessary condition for beginning. It does not mean that confirmation alone guarantees every other condition is safe.

### Prepositions and the patterns that verbs require

English verbs select particular constructions. Translating a preposition word for word often produces a phrase that is understandable but awkward or misleading.

| Revise for the intended meaning | Use | What to notice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| explain me the problem | explain the problem to me | *explain* does not normally take the same double-object pattern as *give* |
| discuss about the scene | discuss the scene | no *about* after *discuss* in this pattern |
| listen the recording | listen to the recording | *listen to*, but *hear the recording* |
| depend from the setting | depend on the setting | the conventional preposition is *on* |
| responsible of sound | responsible for sound | *responsible for* a task |
| arrive to the studio | arrive at the studio | *arrive at* a building; *arrive in* a city |
| in Monday | on Monday | days use *on*; clock times generally use *at* |
| until Friday, meaning a latest completion time | by Friday | *by* gives a deadline; *until* describes continuation to a boundary |

Learn useful units such as **connect A to B**, **replace A with B**, **convert A into B**, **prevent A from happening**, **ask someone to do something**, and **suggest doing something**. They help you explain the technical transformations in this book.

Particle verbs also require care: **Turn the recorder off / Turn off the recorder**, but **Turn it off**, not *Turn off it*. *Plug it in* and *back it up* follow the same pronoun placement pattern in these uses.

### Adjectives, adverbs and the noun phrase

Use **a sensitive microphone**, not *a sensible microphone*, to refer to sensitivity. Use **the microphone works well**, not *works good*, in neutral professional prose. An adjective describes a noun or a state after a linking verb; an adverb can describe an action: **The picture looks good**, but **The operator framed the shot carefully**.

Distinguish **bored/boring**, **interested/interesting** and **confused/confusing**. **The explanation is confusing** attributes an effect to the explanation. **I am confused by the explanation** describes your response. *I am boring* does not mean *dolgčas mi je*.

English adjectives normally precede the noun: **a damaged cable**, **an analogue recorder**, **two spare batteries**. A noun used as a modifier is also common: **a camera battery**, **a recording level**, **a signal path**. Do not assume that every pair of nouns requires an apostrophe. The relationship can be classificatory rather than possessive.

Long noun strings can hide relationships. **Camera battery charging station maintenance instructions** is harder to read than **instructions for maintaining the camera-battery charging station**. In a safety-critical explanation, a short clause can be better still.

### Words that look familiar but do different work

Use the following as contrasts, not as a one-word translation dictionary. The right choice depends on the intended sense.

| English expression | Intended meaning | Contrast worth checking |
| --- | --- | --- |
| actual; actually | real; in fact | *aktualen* often needs *current* or *topical* |
| current | belonging to the present time | current version = sedanja/aktualna različica |
| eventually | in the end, after time or difficulty | *eventualno* meaning possibly needs *possibly* or *perhaps* |
| sensible | reasonable, showing good judgement | *sensitive* concerns susceptibility or responsiveness |
| control | regulate, govern or operate | *kontrolirati* may instead mean *check*, *inspect* or *monitor* |
| realise | become aware of; sometimes achieve | for carrying out a plan, *carry out*, *implement* or *produce* may be more precise |
| evidence | material supporting a claim | *evidenca* may mean a *record*, *register* or *log* |
| sympathetic | understanding another person's feelings | *simpatičen* may need *likeable*, *pleasant* or *charming* |
| possibly | perhaps; it may be possible | not the same as *eventually* |
| take; record; shoot | distinct recording actions in context | say *take a photograph*, *record sound*, *shoot a scene* |

The word **camera** covers both still and moving-image equipment in English. Specify **still camera**, **film camera**, **video camera** or **digital cinema camera** when the distinction matters. In a filmmaking context, *film camera* may mean a camera using photographic film, not just any camera used to make a film.

**Actually, the file is ready** often corrects an assumption. It is not a neutral substitute for **At present, the file is ready**. **We eventually found the fault** means that the fault was found after some process or delay, not that finding it is merely a possibility.

### Reference, articles and commas can change a technical instruction

Compare:

> **The cameras that have charged batteries are ready.**  
> **The cameras, which have charged batteries, are ready.**

The first identifies a subset by its batteries. The second supplies supplementary information about the already identified cameras. Do not remove or add commas without checking whether the claim about the whole set is intended.

In **The operator told the editor that she had replaced the card**, *she* may be unclear. Repeating the relevant role is better than leaving responsibility ambiguous: **The operator told the editor, “I have replaced the card.”** Alternatively, rewrite the report to identify who acted without changing its meaning.

Avoid separating a subject from its predicate with a comma just because the subject is long: **The memory card containing yesterday's interviews is in the case.** An inserted supplementary phrase needs paired punctuation, but a long subject alone does not.

### A worked revision of a production email

The following message deliberately combines several problems:

> Dear all,  
> Yesterday I have checked the equipments. Is missing one cable and I don't know where is the adapter. Please explain me, if we must to order new one. I wait your answer since Monday. We need it until Friday.  
> Best, Maja

For the intended meaning—yesterday's check, a missing adapter, an unanswered earlier request and a Friday deadline—write:

> Dear all,  
> I checked the equipment yesterday. One cable is missing, and I do not know where the adapter is. Could you confirm whether we need to order a replacement adapter? I have been waiting for a reply to Monday's request. We need the replacement by Friday.  
> Best, Maja

**Checked** fits the finished time *yesterday*. **Equipment** is uncountable. **One cable is missing** has a clear subject. **Where the adapter is** has embedded question order. **Need to order** avoids *must to*. **A replacement adapter** makes the object explicit rather than leaving *new one* underspecified. **Have been waiting** connects the earlier request to the present. **By Friday** expresses the latest required arrival time.

The revision also reveals a factual question: does the sender need a replacement adapter, a cable, or both? A proofreader should query that ambiguity rather than invent a purchase. Language correction must preserve the production facts.

### Pronunciation: make the distinction audible

Knowing the right word includes recognising it when somebody else says it and being able to say it clearly yourself. On a set, *thirteen* and *thirty*, *code* and *coat*, or *live* and *leave* can be different instructions. Pronunciation therefore belongs beside grammar and terminology, not in a competition to sound like a particular nationality.

The priorities below are practical checkpoints for learners of English, with particular attention to Slovene and some other Slavic-language backgrounds. **Slavic languages do not have one shared sound system.** A contrast that troubles one learner may be easy for another. Even within Slovene, dialect, previous learning and experience with English matter. These are useful targets to check, not a frequency-ranked list or a diagnosis based on someone's first language.

Research specifically on Slovene learners supports comparing the two sound systems, but also warns against predicting every difficulty from that comparison alone. See Šuštaršič's [contrastive study](https://www.internationalphoneticassociation.org/icphs-proceedings/ICPhS1999/p14_0763.html) and Stopar's [study of English-vowel perception](https://hrcak.srce.hr/231459). The explanations and production-related drills below are original course material, added to the book-based language chapters; they are not reproduced exercises from *Cambridge English for the Media*.

#### Read the pronunciation, not just the spelling

A **phoneme** is a sound category that can distinguish words in a language. A **minimal pair** contains two words differing in one phoneme: *ship* and *sheep*, for example. A **syllable** is a spoken unit organised around a syllabic centre, usually a vowel. Letters, phonemes and syllables are different things: *shot* has four letters, three phonemes in the model used here, and one syllable.

Slashes enclose a broad pronunciation model: **/ʃɒt/**. The sign **/ˈ/** comes immediately before the primarily stressed syllable; **/ˌ/** marks secondary stress. **/ː/** is a length mark within the transcription system, not an instruction to hold a vowel for a fixed number of milliseconds. **/ə/** is schwa, a weak central vowel. The **/j/** in *yes* is a consonant like Slovene *j*, not the first sound of English *job*. English **/ʃ, ʒ, tʃ, dʒ/** are approximately the sound categories represented by Slovene *š, ž, č, dž*, although their exact realisation need not be identical.

Unless labelled otherwise, the transcriptions here use a conventional broad British-English dictionary model. They are learning aids, not a narrow transcription of every speaker. General American and other established varieties can differ in vowels, /r/, stress or individual words without being wrong. British *go* /ɡəʊ/ and American /ɡoʊ/, for example, are both appropriate models. Choose a dictionary recording for the word and meaning you need, and compare your production with that recording. Do not invent a pronunciation by reading an English word according to Slovene spelling rules.

#### Vowels: a different sound, not simply a longer one

For many learners, the difficulty is hearing a distinction before it is producing it. A familiar first-language vowel can seem to cover two English categories. Listen to contrasting words, then compare the tongue and jaw positions as well as their duration. **Length alone is not a reliable explanation of the English vowel system.** The following broad contrasts are useful starting points, with actual pronunciation depending on accent and context.

| Contrast | Words to compare | What to attend to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| /ɪ/ and /iː/ | *ship–sheep; fill–feel; bit–beat; pitch–peach* | The vowels differ in quality as well as typical duration. Simply stretching the vowel of *ship* does not reliably produce *sheep*. |
| /e/ and /æ/ | *pen–pan; bed–bad; said–sad* | In this model, /æ/ has a more open jaw and lower tongue position than /e/. Keep *a bad edit* distinct from *a bed edit* even though context may make one unlikely. |
| /æ/ and /ʌ/ | *cap–cup; track–truck; bag–bug* | Both are relatively open, but the tongue position differs. Do not collapse them into one Slovene *a*-like category. |
| /ʊ/ and /uː/ | *full–fool; pull–pool* | Compare the quality and movement of the vowel, not just lip rounding or duration. The exact position of /uː/ varies considerably across English accents. |
| /ɒ/ and /əʊ/ in the British model | *not–note; cod–code* | /əʊ/ involves a glide. American models use different vowel realisations for these sets; preserve the contrast rather than copying one IPA symbol across accents. |
| Common diphthongs | *frame* /eɪ/; *white* /aɪ/; *sound* /aʊ/; *noise* /ɔɪ/ | Let the vowel move within one syllable. A diphthong is not two separately counted syllables. |

A useful progression is **word → contrasting word → phrase → ordinary explanation**: *fill–feel; fill the frame; feel the vibration; explain what the camera records*. In *live transmission*, *live* has /aɪ/; the verb in *I live in Ljubljana* has /ɪ/. They share spelling, not pronunciation. The verb *leave* has /iː/. Check meaning before deciding how a written word should sound.

Stopar's [2019 experiment](https://hrcak.srce.hr/231459) is the source for the Slovene-specific discussion of vowel perception. The table is a teaching selection of contrasts, not a claim that all participants or all Slavic speakers confuse every pair.

#### /w/ and /v/: watch the lips and teeth

For English **/v/**, the upper teeth approach the lower lip and the escaping air produces friction, normally with voicing. For **/w/**, round the lips and move smoothly into the following vowel without using that teeth–lip friction. The back of the tongue also participates in /w/; it is not merely an unusually short /v/.

Practise **west–vest, wine–vine, wet–vet**. Then use **white balance, wide shot, waveform, viewfinder, video, voice-over**. In **a wide view**, the first word begins with /w/ and the second with /v/. In **a very wide view**, all three consonant positions need attention.

Do not say that Slovene speakers have never produced a [w]-like sound. Slovene *v* has different realisations in different environments. The English task is to make the /w/–/v/ distinction where it separates English words and to avoid overcorrecting every *v* into /w/. Metruk's [study of Slovak learners](https://jle.hse.ru/en/article/view/7384) documents substitutions in both directions; that Slovak finding is not automatically a measurement of the present Slovene class.

#### The two “th” sounds: /θ/ and /ð/

In **think, three, thin, both**, *th* represents **/θ/**. In **this, that, these, other**, it represents **/ð/**. For both, bring the tongue tip lightly to the upper teeth or just between the teeth and let air continue through the narrow opening. Do not close the airflow completely as for /t/ or /d/. In /ð/, add voicing; in /θ/, there is no voiced buzz. There is no need to push the tongue far out or bite it.

Compare **thin–tin, three–tree, thought–taught** and **then–den, they–day, breathe–breed**. These comparisons identify specific contrasts; they do not imply that every regional English substitution is a learner error. For our practice model, keep *three takes* distinct from *tree takes* and keep the final sound audible in *both*.

Spelling still needs checking. **Breath** /breθ/ is a noun; **breathe** /briːð/ is a verb, so both the vowel and final consonant differ. **Thomas** begins with /t/, despite its spelling. Use the dictionary's entries for [three](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/pronunciation/english/three), [this](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/pronunciation/english/this), [breath](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/pronunciation/english/breath) and [breathe](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/pronunciation/english/breathe) to hear the models.

#### Word endings: keep the contrast, not an exaggerated buzz

In Slovene and several other Slavic languages, word-final obstruents can be devoiced. English word-final contrasts require different treatment. Compare **cap–cab, coat–code, back–bag, safe–save, ice–eyes**. A word ending written with *d* or *g* should not automatically be treated as its *t* or *k* counterpart.
